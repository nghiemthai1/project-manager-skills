# Stakeholder Engagement Advisor

## Purpose

Choose the next engagement action that addresses a stakeholder's actual concern. Use when a status email is not resolving a disagreement or a necessary participant is disengaged. The goal is a workable decision with clear authority, not persuasion at any cost.

## Input

Bring the stakeholder's role, the decision or outcome at stake, their actual statements, prior engagement, and constraints.

Example: "Operations will not accept handover. Help me understand whether to negotiate, change the plan, or escalate."

Separate observed statements from your interpretation. Avoid guessing motives from role or tone.

Use context already supplied. If inputs are incomplete, distinguish useful draft work from decisions that require missing evidence. Mark unknowns explicitly; never fill them with example data.

## Key Concepts

### Diagnose before choosing a message

Disagreement may concern evidence, priorities, authority, capacity, or trust. Each requires a different response. More reassuring language will not resolve missing restore evidence. An executive escalation will not answer an unclear acceptance test.

Use interest-based problem solving: identify the underlying concern, distinguish it from a proposed position, develop feasible options, and compare them against explicit criteria. Use stakeholder influence/impact assessment to select who must participate, not to decide whose concern is legitimate.

### Why this works

Matching the intervention to the concern makes the next conversation productive. A bounded ask with relevant evidence reduces back-and-forth. Recording authority prevents an apparent agreement from being mistaken for approval.

## Application

1. Establish the decision at stake and the stakeholder's evidenced concern. If missing, ask for their words before inferring intent.
2. Ask the next discriminator: evidence gap, conflicting priority, missing authority, resource constraint, or damaged trust. Normally use at most three to five material questions.
3. Offer two or three feasible interventions with tradeoffs, such as evidence review, joint option design, authority clarification, or escalation.
4. Recommend one next action, intended outcome, participants, and decision criteria. Include what would change the recommendation.
5. Draft a short message or agenda using facts and a specific ask. Label it a draft; do not imply it has been sent or accepted.
6. Define follow-up evidence: agreement recorded, question answered, owner assigned, or decision escalated. Update the stakeholder map when understanding changes.

Use the [artifact template](template.md). Keep the deliverable concise; retain the reasoning needed to explain its consequential choices.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Mind-reading:** "she wants to block us" substitutes for evidence. Quote the concern and test the interpretation.
- **Communication as camouflage:** reassuring wording hides missing acceptance evidence. Address the underlying gap.
- **Escalation first:** hierarchy is used before clarifying a solvable question. Escalate when authority or unresolved consequence requires it.
- **Agreement inflation:** a friendly conversation becomes formal approval. Record exactly what was agreed and by whom.
