# Project team leadership methods

Use this reference when the situation involves recurring missed commitments, a material delegation, a feedback conversation or a project-based development plan.

## Diagnose before choosing the response

Test the delivery system in this order:

1. **Outcome:** Was the required result and reason clear?
2. **Boundary:** Were included work, exclusions and acceptance explicit?
3. **Commitment:** Did the contributor actually accept ownership and timing?
4. **Authority:** Could the contributor make the expected decisions?
5. **Capacity:** Was availability accepted after other obligations and support work?
6. **Inputs:** Were dependencies, access, tools and information usable?
7. **Capability:** Did the person have or receive the needed skill and context?
8. **Feedback:** Did the leader identify drift early and respond?
9. **Repeated choice:** After the preceding conditions were addressed, did the agreed behavior still fail?

Record evidence for each. Do not leap from a missed date to motive. When several people show the same failure, inspect priorities, incentives, process and leadership before individualizing the problem.

## Build a delegation brief

A strong delegation defines:

- why the outcome matters;
- the result and acceptance evidence;
- included and excluded work;
- decisions the owner may make and decisions reserved;
- constraints, risks and assumptions;
- confirmed capacity, tools, information and dependencies;
- stakeholders and interfaces;
- checkpoints sized to risk and readiness;
- early-warning and escalation triggers;
- the read-back that confirms acceptance.

### Match review to readiness and risk

Use closer review when the work is novel, irreversible, regulated, highly coupled or performed without demonstrated readiness. Use wider autonomy when the person has relevant evidence and the work is reversible. State what result will reduce or increase review. Closer review should inspect decisions and evidence, not prescribe every action.

## Prepare factual feedback

### Purpose

Connect the conversation to a shared project result or working agreement. Avoid opening with a personality judgment.

### Observation

Use a specific action, omission, commitment or pattern with date and source. Separate what you observed from what you inferred.

### Impact

Explain the consequence for schedule, rework, risk, another team, customer, trust or decision quality. Avoid exaggeration and universal words such as “always.”

### Perspective

Ask what the contributor saw, which constraints changed and what support was missing. Correct the working diagnosis when new evidence warrants it.

### Next behavior

Define the observable change, support, timing and checkpoint. Ask the contributor to read back the commitment and raise feasibility concerns.

Feedback may be firm while remaining respectful. Psychological safety means bad news can be raised without retaliation; it does not remove accountability for commitments.

## Coach a project skill

Choose one skill that affects the project, such as dependency negotiation, risk disclosure, estimation, review preparation, decision framing, technical demonstration or stakeholder facilitation.

Use a short loop:

1. define what competent performance looks like;
2. observe a current example;
3. ask the contributor to explain their reasoning;
4. model or teach only the missing part;
5. assign a real but bounded practice opportunity;
6. observe and give timely feedback;
7. review evidence and choose the next level of autonomy.

Project coaching develops work capability. Formal career goals, promotion readiness and employment evaluation remain with the authorized line manager, though project evidence may inform them through the agreed route.

## Respond to recurring missed commitments

Use an escalation ladder based on evidence, not irritation:

| Condition | Project response |
|---|---|
| First material miss with unclear delegation | Clarify and re-contract the work |
| Confirmed obstacle or allocation conflict | Remove obstacle or obtain an authority decision |
| Observable behavior affected work and was not surfaced | Give direct feedback and agree an early-warning behavior |
| Specific skill gap | Create a bounded coaching and practice loop |
| Repeated miss after clarity, support and accepted checkpoints | Protect the project and involve the actual line/resource/supplier authority |
| Conduct, safety, retaliation or protected matter | Use the applicable authorized policy route immediately |

Escalation records the project facts, support provided, current consequence and decision needed. It should not contain amateur diagnoses or punitive recommendations outside authority.

## Design a team-development plan

Use patterns across the team to select one or two capabilities that will improve delivery. Examples include earlier risk disclosure, sharper decision framing, better interface agreements or stronger review evidence.

For each capability define:

- the observed project pattern;
- target behavior;
- a practice embedded in real work;
- peer, expert or leader support;
- evidence and review cadence;
- who may approve time or formal development;
- a stop, revise or scale trigger.

Do not create a generic training list. A project-development action earns its place when it changes a recurring delivery mechanism.

## Review fairly

At each checkpoint:

1. compare the agreed evidence with the observed result;
2. distinguish improvement, unchanged behavior and changed conditions;
3. recognize specific progress;
4. adjust support or autonomy;
5. update the project plan prospectively;
6. escalate only the remaining decision or authority gap.

Preserve the chronology. Support does not erase prior impact, and prior failure does not justify ignoring current improvement.
