# Stakeholder Map

## Purpose

Identify whose decisions, work, or exposure shape the project, then choose proportionate engagement. Use at initiation and when scope or organizational context changes. A map supports engagement; it is not a ranking of people's worth or a substitute for decision authority.

## Input

Bring the project outcome, affected workflows, decision bodies, delivery teams, users, vendors, and support responsibilities.

Example: "Map who must participate in the Northstar cutover decision and who will live with the result."

With an incomplete roster, generate stakeholder categories to validate, not invented named participants.

Use context already supplied. If inputs are incomplete, distinguish useful draft work from decisions that require missing evidence. Mark unknowns explicitly; never fill them with example data.

## Key Concepts

### Power and interest, plus impact

A power/interest map helps allocate engagement effort: who can materially affect a decision, and who has a strong stake in it? Add impact because highly affected people may have little formal power. A low-power user group can expose an acceptance failure executives never see.

Distinguish formal authority, practical influence, affectedness, and current stance. A senior title does not prove release acceptance authority; a resistant stakeholder may be surfacing legitimate operating risk.

### Why this works

The map turns "keep everyone aligned" into specific engagement needs and unresolved authority questions. It is a hypothesis to validate through conversation, not a permanent label applied to a person.

Use a simple table for a small project. A quadrant picture is optional; the decision and engagement implications matter more than visual placement.

## Application

1. Start from affected workflows and project decisions, then list the people or groups connected to each. Include operators, support users, security, vendors, and less-visible affected groups.
2. Record authority, influence, interest, impact, stance evidence, and confidence. Leave unverified assessments tentative.
3. Check for missing acceptance or benefit owners. Use actual governance records rather than inferring from job titles.
4. Choose an engagement purpose for each group: decide, co-design, validate, advise, or receive updates.
5. Assign a relationship owner and next contact where authorized. Draft outreach if requested; do not send it without authority.
6. Review when scope, leadership, or impact changes. Feed confirmed needs into the communication and engagement plans.

Use the [artifact template](template.md). Keep the deliverable concise; retain the reasoning needed to explain its consequential choices.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Org chart equals map:** frontline users are omitted. Start from affected workflows as well as reporting lines.
- **Resistance equals obstruction:** legitimate service risk becomes a personality label. Record the concern and evidence instead.
- **Power equals acceptance:** the loudest executive is assumed to approve security. Verify formal authority.
- **Permanent quadrant:** influence and impact never change after kickoff. Reassess at meaningful project changes.
