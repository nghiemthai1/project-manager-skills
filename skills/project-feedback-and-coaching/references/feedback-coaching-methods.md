# Feedback and coaching methods

Use this reference when a project situation needs factual feedback, a project-skill practice loop or both.

## Distinguish the interventions

| Need | Primary intervention | Evidence of completion |
|---|---|---|
| An observable behavior or omission has material project impact | Feedback conversation | Shared factual record and an explicit next behavior |
| A specific project skill or judgment needs development | Coaching loop | Improved performance on a bounded practice and later real work |
| The outcome or commitment was unclear | Renewed delegation | Accepted outcome, authority, capacity and timing |
| A repeated pattern affects several people | System correction or retrospective | Tested team-level change |
| Competing interests prevent agreement | Negotiation or mediation | Bounded agreement, decision or unresolved record |

Do not call every difficult conversation coaching. Do not use feedback to conceal an allocation, dependency or authority problem.

## Prepare factual feedback

### Purpose

Connect the conversation to a shared project result or working agreement. Avoid opening with a personality judgment.

### Observation

Use a specific action, omission, commitment or pattern with date and source. Separate observation from inference. Preserve contrary evidence and uncertainty.

### Impact

Explain the consequence for schedule, rework, risk, another team, customer, trust or decision quality. Avoid exaggeration and universal words such as “always.”

### Perspective

Ask what the contributor saw, which constraints changed and what support was missing. Correct the working diagnosis when new evidence warrants it.

### Next behavior

Define the observable change, support, timing and checkpoint. Ask the contributor to read back the result and raise feasibility concerns.

Feedback may be firm while remaining respectful. Psychological safety means bad news can be raised without retaliation; it does not remove accountability for agreed project behavior.

## Coach a project skill

Choose one skill that affects delivery, such as dependency negotiation, risk disclosure, estimation, review preparation, decision framing, technical demonstration or stakeholder facilitation.

Use a short loop:

1. define competent project performance;
2. observe a current example;
3. ask the contributor to explain their reasoning;
4. model or teach only the missing part;
5. assign a real but bounded practice opportunity;
6. observe and give timely feedback;
7. review evidence and choose the next level of autonomy.

Project coaching develops work capability. Formal career goals, promotion readiness and employment evaluation remain with the authorized line manager, though project evidence may be shared through the agreed route.

## Design a team-level capability response

If several people show the same pattern, avoid creating identical individual coaching plans before checking the project system. A team-level improvement may use a retrospective, working-agreement reset, shared example or peer review.

For each selected capability define:

- the observed project pattern;
- target behavior;
- practice embedded in real work;
- peer, expert or leader support;
- evidence and review cadence;
- who may approve time or formal development;
- a stop, revise or scale trigger.

Use an individual record only when the observation, support and privacy boundary are appropriately individual.

## Review fairly

At each checkpoint:

1. compare the agreed behavior or skill evidence with the result;
2. distinguish improvement, unchanged behavior and changed conditions;
3. recognize specific progress;
4. adjust support or practice difficulty;
5. close the loop when the evidence is sufficient;
6. change route when coaching is not addressing the mechanism.

Preserve the factual chronology. Prior impact remains part of the project record, while current improvement must also be recognized.
