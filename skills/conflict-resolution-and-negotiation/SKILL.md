# Conflict Resolution and Negotiation

## Purpose

Turn project disagreement into a bounded resolution process. Diagnose what is actually in conflict, distinguish facts from interpretations and positions from interests, identify who may decide, then choose direct dialogue, structured negotiation, facilitated mediation or escalation. Produce a conflict assessment, negotiation boundaries, conversation plan, agreement or decision record, and follow-up checkpoints.

Use this skill when competing interests or damaged working relationships prevent a project decision or commitment. Use stakeholder engagement for a one-sided outreach plan when no active conflict needs resolution. Use an escalation brief when the issue is already understood and only an authorized decision is missing.

This skill does not confer line-management, HR, legal, safety, acceptance or commercial authority. A project manager may coordinate the process and document outcomes while another role owns employment action, formal acceptance, contract interpretation or the final decision.

## Input

Works best with the issue, parties, current positions, observed events, affected project outcome, decision deadline, attempted conversations, decision rights and non-negotiable constraints. Include relevant records such as requirements, acceptance criteria, contracts, policies, decisions, messages or meeting notes.

Use supplied material before asking questions. Preserve exact identifiers and dates. Mark motives, alternatives and authority as unknown when they are not evidenced. If only one party's account is available, label it as one perspective rather than a settled fact.

With partial input, create a useful preparation draft and ask only questions that change the route or boundary:

1. What concrete decision, behavior or resource is disputed, and what project consequence is occurring?
2. What has each party actually said or done, and what is interpretation or inferred motive?
3. What does each party need to protect, and which constraints are fixed, flexible or unknown?
4. Who has authority for the disputed decision, acceptance or people matter?
5. What has already been tried, and is direct dialogue safe and feasible?

## Key Concepts

### Classify the conflict before solving it

Similar symptoms need different responses.

| Primary condition | Evidence pattern | Suitable route |
|---|---|---|
| Information gap | Parties rely on different facts, versions or criteria | Joint fact check, then direct dialogue |
| Interest or tradeoff conflict | Both positions are understandable but cannot all be satisfied as stated | Structured negotiation |
| Process or role conflict | Ownership, acceptance or decision rights are ambiguous | Clarify governance before bargaining |
| Relationship rupture | Trust, communication or repeated behavior blocks problem solving | Direct repair or neutral mediation |
| Authority deadlock | The parties understand the tradeoff but cannot decide within delegation | Evidence-based escalation |
| Conduct, safety or retaliation concern | A fair voluntary conversation cannot be assumed | Follow policy and involve the authorized line, HR, legal or safety role |

Do not reduce every conflict to personality. Schedule pressure, unclear acceptance, incompatible incentives, missing capacity and shadow governance often create predictable friction.

### Separate positions, interests and constraints

A position is the stated demand. An interest is the need or concern behind it. A constraint is a real limit such as law, policy, funding, capacity, quality threshold or delegated authority. Treat a claimed constraint as evidence to verify, not automatically as an immovable fact.

Map each party's stated position, evidenced interests, possible interests marked as hypotheses, constraints, contribution to the problem and acceptable evidence. Look for shared interests before generating options.

### Know the no-agreement alternatives

For a material negotiation, identify each party's best available alternative if no agreement is reached. For internal projects this may be retain the current baseline, pause work, use another resource, narrow a recommendation, or escalate to the actual decider. Test whether the alternative is executable; a hope or threat is not an alternative.

Define the reservation boundary: the least acceptable outcome before the no-agreement alternative is preferable. Do not fabricate leverage, bluff about alternatives or pressure someone to waive a mandatory control. A zone of possible agreement exists only where the parties can accept the same package within their real constraints and authority.

### Match the process to the situation

1. **Direct dialogue** fits a bounded disagreement where the parties can speak safely and the decision rights are clear.
2. **Structured negotiation** fits a multi-variable tradeoff where interests, alternatives and objective criteria can produce a package.
3. **Facilitated mediation** fits stalled dialogue, strained trust, power imbalance that can be managed, or several parties needing a neutral process.
4. **Authority escalation** fits a known tradeoff beyond delegation, a required control exception, or a deadlock after a fair process.

Mediation is voluntary problem solving, not a substitute for investigation or an authority decision. The facilitator controls the process, not the outcome. If impartiality cannot be maintained, use another facilitator.

### Agreement is not the same as authorization

Record whether the outcome is a working agreement, recommendation, conditional agreement, authorized decision or unresolved item. Name the actual decision scope and authority. Silence, meeting attendance and a friendly closing do not establish agreement.

## Application

Use a single-shot draft when the user supplies enough evidence. In guided mode, ask one material question at a time and normally stop after the five input questions. Then present numbered routes with tradeoffs and recommend one.

### 1. Establish readiness and boundaries

Check whether direct participation is safe, voluntary and within policy. Identify confidentiality expectations before private conversations; never promise confidentiality that the facilitator cannot keep. Route allegations, harassment, discrimination, threats, retaliation, employment action and legal disputes through the applicable authorized process.

Name the outcome this process may reach and what it cannot decide. Keep project coordination separate from line-management authority.

### 2. Build the conflict assessment

Create a chronology of observed events and source references. Separate:

- facts and supplied statements;
- each party's interpretation;
- unknowns and disputed claims;
- project impact and urgency;
- decision, acceptance and escalation rights;
- previous resolution attempts.

State the shared problem neutrally. Prefer “the release lacks agreed recovery evidence” to “security is blocking.”

### 3. Map interests, alternatives and criteria

For each party, record the stated position, interests, constraints, no-agreement alternative and reservation boundary. Mark estimates of another party's interests or alternatives as hypotheses for validation. Choose objective criteria before choosing an option: approved requirements, service levels, contractual terms, capacity evidence, risk limits, policy or comparable precedent.

Generate several packages where variables can trade across scope, timing, sequence, resources, evidence, risk treatment or review cadence. Include the unchanged course and no-agreement outcome where material. Show who benefits, who carries each cost or risk, and which authority is required.

For deeper preparation, use [the conflict and negotiation methods](references/conflict-and-negotiation-methods.md).

### 4. Prepare and run the conversation

Use the [artifact template](template.md) to prepare:

- a neutral opening and shared purpose;
- observable facts and source boundaries;
- questions that test interests and constraints;
- options to explore without premature commitment;
- pause, caucus or escalation triggers;
- an explicit close and read-back.

Invite correction. Paraphrase each perspective and ask whether it was captured accurately. Generate options before evaluating them. Test a complete package against interests, criteria, authority and the no-agreement alternatives.

Drafting a message or agenda does not send or schedule it. External communication still requires the user's authorization.

### 5. Record the outcome and follow through

Document exact terms, scope, owner, date, conditions, authority, evidence and review trigger. Preserve dissent and unresolved items. If an authorized decision follows an unresolved negotiation, record the decision separately from the parties' agreement.

Set checkpoints that test behavior or delivery results, not just meeting completion. Reopen the agreement when a named assumption fails, evidence changes, a commitment is missed, harm recurs or the decision boundary changes.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Starting with compromise:** splitting the difference before finding interests can preserve the wrong constraint and satisfy nobody. Map interests, alternatives and criteria first.
- **Invented motives:** “they only want control” turns an interpretation into fact. Record observed behavior and test the hypothesis with a neutral question.
- **Forced mediation:** a project manager orders people into a confidential-looking process despite a conduct or power concern. Apply policy and use an authorized neutral route.
- **Negotiating mandatory evidence away:** schedule pressure becomes an informal waiver of security, safety or acceptance. Keep the control and its exception authority explicit.
- **False internal BATNA:** “work around them” is treated as leverage even though the receiver or approver remains required. Test whether the alternative is executable and authorized.
- **Agreement by attendance:** meeting notes say “all aligned” without an exact read-back or decision. Record each term and confirmation state.
- **No follow-through:** a conversation improves tone but the same missed handoff recurs. Add observable commitments, owners and review triggers.
